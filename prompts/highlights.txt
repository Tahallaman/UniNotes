You are choosing which parts of a recorded lecture are worth watching.

The student already has written notes for this lecture. They are not asking what
was said — they can read that. They are asking where watching beats reading:
where an idea gets built in front of you, where a worked example goes wrong and
gets fixed, where the lecturer says the thing that makes it click.

Your job is to find every such span and mark where it starts and ends.

WHAT TO KEEP

- An idea being built: a derivation, a proof, a diagram drawn while talking, a
  model assembled a piece at a time.
- Worked examples, especially ones with a wrong turn that gets corrected.
- Why something exists, what it is for, or why the obvious approach fails.
- Anything flagged as mattering: "this is examinable", "people always get this
  wrong", "this is the key idea".
- A correction to something said earlier in the lecture.
- An answer to a question from the room that adds something the lecture had not
  already said.

WHAT TO CUT

- Admin: deadlines, assignment logistics, room and timetable changes, marks.
  These belong in the notes, not in the student's ears.
- A slide read aloud with nothing added.
- Recaps — of last week, or of the last ten minutes.
- Anecdotes, asides and tangents, however good.
- Dead air, setup, waiting for people, technical problems.
- Questions from the room whose answer repeats what was just said.

THE SHAPE OF A SPAN

- It begins where a thought begins and ends where that thought is finished. Not
  a sentence, not a slide, not a fixed length.
- Roughly 45 seconds to 4 minutes. Under 30 seconds is not a span, it is a jump
  cut, and a reel of those is unwatchable. If two good moments are less than
  half a minute apart, make them one span.
- Start slightly early rather than slightly late. Landing mid-sentence is worse
  than hearing five seconds of run-up.

COVERAGE

Work through the whole lecture. A lecture has an arc, and five spans from the
first fifteen minutes is a failure however good they are. If a section of the
lecture genuinely contains nothing worth watching, leave it out — a thin lecture
should produce a short list, and padding it is the worst thing you can do here.

IMPORTANCE

Score every span 1 to 5. The score is what decides whether it survives into a
short reel, so spread the range and mean it:

  5  Without this, the lecture is not understood. The central derivation, the
     idea the rest is built on, the thing that will be examined.
  4  Substantial. A worked example, a second key idea, the answer to the
     question a careful student would have.
  3  Worth watching if there is room. Useful detail, a good aside on method.
  2  Marginal. Watch only if watching almost all of it.
  1  Barely worth it. Prefer to leave it out entirely.

Do not give everything a 4. If more than about a third of your spans are 5s, you
have not chosen.

TIMES

- Every start and end must be a time that appears in the transcript below.
  Do not invent times, do not round, do not interpolate.
- Times are MM:SS, or H:MM:SS past an hour.
- Spans must not overlap, and must be in time order.

REASONS

One line per span saying what happens in it, concretely enough to be worth
reading on its own. "Derives the two-bit saturating counter and shows why one
bit thrashes" is useful. "Important concept" and "the lecturer explains the
material" are not — they say nothing the timestamp did not.

OUTPUT

Return JSON and nothing else. No prose before or after, no code fence, no
commentary.

[
  { "start": "04:12", "end": "07:40", "weight": 5, "why": "Derives the two-bit saturating counter and shows why one bit thrashes on a loop" },
  { "start": "18:05", "end": "20:31", "weight": 3, "why": "Works the branch-history example on the board, including the off-by-one he corrects" }
]

If the lecture genuinely contains nothing worth watching, return an empty array.
That is a valid answer and a more useful one than a list of filler.
