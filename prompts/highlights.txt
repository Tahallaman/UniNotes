You are cutting a recorded lecture into a highlights reel.

Think of what a good editor does to a two-hour keynote to make a ten-minute
recap: they do not pick five long chapters. They cut constantly — a sentence
here, a claim there, the ten seconds where the number is said, the twenty where
the point actually lands — and the result plays as a fast, dense run through
everything that mattered.

Do that to this lecture. The brief below says how long this particular reel's
cuts should be and how much of the lecture it should add up to. Everything else
on this page applies to every reel.

USE THE NOTES TO DECIDE, THE TRANSCRIPT TO TIME

You are given two things and they do different jobs.

The **notes** were written from this recording and already say what mattered in
it — the definitions, the figures, the worked examples, the conclusions. They
are your list of what to look for. Work down them.

The **transcript** is where each of those things was actually said. It is the
only source of times, and it is the only place a start or an end may come from.

So the method is: take a point from the notes, find the moment in the transcript
where it is stated, and cut a span around exactly that. Do not go looking for
your own idea of what a lecture should contain — the notes already did that
work, from the recording itself. If the notes carry a timestamp, treat it as a
strong hint about where to look, not as a boundary: it marks roughly where a
point was made, and you are choosing precisely where the sentence starts and
finishes.

Anything the notes are quiet about is probably not worth cutting. Anything the
notes give a heading, a figure, or a bullet to is probably worth at least one
span.

CUT SHORT AND CUT OFTEN

- One idea, claim, number or definition per span. Not a topic. Not a section.
  Not "the part about baselines" — the sentence where he says what a baseline
  *is*, and separately the sentence where he says what happens if you get it
  wrong. Those are two spans.
- Prefer two short spans over one long one, always. If two good moments are
  twenty seconds apart, that is TWO spans with the twenty seconds cut out, not
  one span with the padding left in.
- Start on the point, not on its run-up. Cut the "so, um, what we're going to
  look at now is" and keep what follows.
- Be exhaustive rather than selective. Go through in order and take everything
  that qualifies; a span you are unsure about goes in with a low score rather
  than being left out.

COVERAGE — THE WHOLE LECTURE, NOT ITS MIDDLE

Work from the first minute to the last. Every two minutes of speech that
contains anything at all should produce at least one span. A reel that stops
paying attention after the halfway mark is a failure however good its first half
is. Before you answer, read back your own list and look at the gaps: a stretch of
three minutes with nothing in it is either genuinely empty — admin, a break, a
tangent — or something you missed.

IT HAS TO PLAY AS ONE THING

The spans will be played back to back, with everything between them gone. So
they are not a list of good moments; they are a cut of the lecture, and someone
watching it start to finish should get the argument in order and come out
understanding the lecture — not a collection of disconnected clips.

That changes some choices:

- Take the sentence that *introduces* a topic as well as the one that lands it.
  Ten seconds of "so the next thing we need is a way to value a human life"
  turns the definition that follows from a fact into a step in a story.
- Keep the connective moments — "which brings us to", "so the problem with that
  is", "and that's why we need". They are short, and they are what makes two
  cuts feel like one argument instead of two clips.
- Watch the joins. If cutting straight from one span to the next would land
  mid-thought or contradict what was just heard, extend one of them by a few
  seconds or take the bridging sentence as its own short span.
- Follow the lecture's own order and structure. Do not reorder, and do not
  cluster everything from one section — the reel should move through the
  lecture at roughly the pace the lecture moves.
- End on an ending. If the lecturer concludes, sums up, or says what it all
  means, that is the last span.

WHAT TO KEEP

- The sentence where a concept is defined, or a term is given its meaning.
- The moment a number, threshold, formula or rule of thumb is stated.
- The step in a derivation or worked example where the reasoning turns.
- Why something exists, what it is for, or why the obvious approach fails.
- Anything flagged as mattering: "this is examinable", "people always get this
  wrong", "this is the key idea".
- A correction to something said earlier.
- The answer to a question from the room, when it adds something new.
- A conclusion, or the "so what this means is…" moment.

WHAT TO CUT

- Admin: deadlines, assignment logistics, room changes, marks.
- A slide read aloud with nothing added.
- Recaps of last week or of the last ten minutes.
- Anecdotes, tangents and jokes, however good.
- Dead air, setup, waiting for people, technical problems.
- Repetition — if the same claim is made twice, keep the clearer one only.

IMPORTANCE

Score every span 1 to 5. If the reel comes out longer than it was asked for, the
lowest scores are dropped first, so the score is what protects the best material:

  5  Without this, the lecture is not understood. The central definition, the
     key number, the idea the rest is built on, the thing that will be examined.
  4  Substantial. A step in the main argument, a worked figure, a real example.
  3  Worth watching if there is room. Useful detail, a good aside on method.
  2  Marginal.
  1  Barely worth it.

Do not give everything a 4. About a tenth of your spans should be 5s — with a
hundred spans that is ten, not forty.

TIMES

- Every start and end must be a time that appears in the transcript below.
  Do not invent times, do not round, do not interpolate.
- Times are MM:SS, or H:MM:SS past an hour.
- Spans must not overlap, and must be in time order.

REASONS

One line per span saying what happens in it, concretely enough to be worth
reading on its own. "States the Value of Statistical Life as $12.5M NZD" is
useful. "Important concept" and "the lecturer explains the material" are not —
they say nothing the timestamp did not.

OUTPUT

Return JSON and nothing else. No prose before or after, no code fence, no
commentary.

[
  { "start": "04:12", "end": "04:31", "weight": 5, "why": "Defines the counterfactual baseline as what happens if you do nothing" },
  { "start": "04:55", "end": "05:12", "weight": 4, "why": "States that getting the baseline wrong flips the sign of the whole NPV" },
  { "start": "18:05", "end": "18:27", "weight": 5, "why": "Gives the Value of Statistical Life as $12.5M NZD and where the figure comes from" }
]

Note the shape of that example: short, adjacent, one claim each — not three
chapters. Match it, at the length the brief asks for.

If the lecture genuinely contains nothing worth watching, return an empty array.
That is a valid answer and a more useful one than a list of filler.
